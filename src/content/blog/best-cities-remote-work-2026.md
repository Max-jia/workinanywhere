I wish someone had told me this when I started: pick the wrong city and you lose more than money. You lose months of your life to loneliness, being broke, or a feeling you can't name. I have done it. More than once.

But the right city? Everything shifts. Your money goes further, you find your crowd, and suddenly this whole "work from anywhere" idea actually holds up.

After three years of nomad hubs, too many Airbnbs to count, and burnout in places everyone else swore by, these are the cities I tell my friends to go to. What follows is based on rent receipts and visa rejections, not Instagram.

---

## The top 5 cities

### 1. Lisbon, Portugal

Lisbon was the first place I landed where I thought: *okay, I could stay here for a while.*

300 days of sun a year, a straightforward digital nomad visa, and a community that feels like a club already in session when you walk into any coworking space in Principe Real. The coffee is excellent, the pasteis de nata are a problem, and the hills will either keep you fit or make you curse every step. Both are valid.

The numbers: you will spend about $2,200 to $2,800 a month for a comfortable solo life. A decent one-bedroom runs $1,200 to $1,800, a coworking desk $150 to $350. The D8 visa requires EUR 3,680 a month in income, active only (freelancers and remote employees, not passive income). Two years, renewable, path to citizenship after five.

The catch: Lisbon is not the budget European gem it was five years ago. Rents have climbed 30 to 40 percent. Locals feel the squeeze, and the city keeps having the same argument about tourism versus livability.

---

### 2. Chiang Mai, Thailand

If you have read anything about digital nomad life, you have heard of Chiang Mai. There is a reason. It is still the best value-for-money nomad hub on the planet, and it is where I send anyone who is just starting out.

You can eat street food that beats most restaurants for $1 to $3 per meal, rent a modern studio for $250 to $400, and join a coworking space on Nimmanhaemin Road for $70 to $150 a month. The nomad community here is huge, about 20,000 people, so you never struggle to find someone to grab dinner with.

The DTV visa gives you 5 years of flexibility, 180-day stays per entry, extendable once per entry for another 180 days. You need 500,000 THB (about $14,500) in savings, and embassies have gotten serious about enforcing the 3-month seasoning requirement. Bitcoin portfolios do not count. Bank statements do.

One thing nobody tells you: February through April is burning season. The air quality becomes genuinely hazardous. Most experienced nomads leave northern Thailand entirely during these months. I learned this the hard way my first year.

### 3. Medellin, Colombia

If someone had told me five years ago that a Colombian city would become one of the world's top nomad destinations, I would have believed them after one week here.

They call it the "City of Eternal Spring" because the temperature hovers between 63 and 83 degrees Fahrenheit year-round. You do not need AC. You do not need heating. You just need a window that opens. El Poblado and Laureles are packed with coworking spaces ($80 to $200 a month) and cafes where you can camp for hours on a single tinto. Your total monthly budget: $1,200 to $1,600.

The visa has a sneaky rule: Colombia requires about $1,400 to $1,450 a month in income (three times minimum wage). But here is the catch: you need to meet that threshold *each of the last 3 months individually*. No averaging across months. Only bank statements count as proof. I know nomads who got rejected because one month dipped slightly below the line. Budget for $1,600 a month as a buffer.

Neighborhood choice is everything. El Poblado and Laureles are safe and expat-friendly. Outside those zones, the experience changes fast. Spanish is not optional. Even basic Spanish makes a real difference in your daily life here.

### 4. Bali (Canggu/Ubud), Indonesia

Bali is less a city choice and more a lifestyle choice. Canggu has surf, sunsets, and a tight community of entrepreneurs. Ubud is the quieter alternative: yoga, rice terraces, and a slower pace.

But before you book that one-way ticket: the E33G Remote Worker Visa requires $60,000 a year in income, the highest threshold of any major nomad visa. As of 2026, Indonesian immigration enforces this with task forces doing spot checks. The old "work on a tourist visa and hope nobody notices" approach does not fly anymore. I have watched people get caught.

Budget $1,200 to $2,000 a month. The real cost is traffic. What looks like a 10-minute drive can easily take 40 minutes. Pick your neighborhood based on where you will actually work, not where the best sunset photos are.

---

### 5. Mexico City, Mexico

CDMX is enormous, culturally overwhelming in the best way, and walkable in the right neighborhoods. Roma Norte and Condesa feel like a European city dropped into Latin America.

The 180-day tourist visa for many passport holders makes this the lowest-friction entry point of any major nomad city. No applications, no income proof, no waiting. Six months, automatically. The trade-off: you cannot stay indefinitely without a proper residency visa. Budget $1,500 to $2,300 a month. And the altitude (7,350 feet) will humble you for the first week. Fatigue, headaches, shortness of breath. Drink twice as much water as you think you need.

## Five more worth your time

Da Nang, Vietnam: $700 to $1,200 a month. Southeast Asia's best beach value, still quiet compared to Bangkok or Bali. 90-day e-visa, great Vietnamese coffee, and fiber internet that is faster than what I had in Lisbon.

Barcelona, Spain: $2,000 to $3,000 a month. Expensive but the lifestyle is hard to beat if you can afford it. Spain's digital nomad visa opens doors to the Beckham Law, a big tax advantage for foreign employees. See my [full Spain visa guide](/blog/spain-digital-nomad-visa-2026) for the 2026 income requirements and application process.

Buenos Aires, Argentina: $1,000 to $1,800 a month. The blue dollar era ended, but BA still gives you a European-feeling city at roughly half the cost of Western capitals.

Prague, Czechia: $1,500 to $2,500 a month. Central European base with fast fiber, a freelancer trade license, and architecture that makes you feel like you are living in a fairy tale.

Penang, Malaysia: $800 to $1,400 a month. The food capital nobody talks about enough. 90-day visa-free, excellent street food, laid-back island pace.

## 5 more rising spots

These are places where the infrastructure keeps improving but the crowds have not shown up yet. If you want to get in before the prices catch up, these are worth a look.

Tbilisi, Georgia: $900 to $1,500 a month. Georgia lets citizens of 95-plus countries stay for a full year visa-free. The old town is a maze of wooden balconies and wine bars, the coworking scene around Fabrika is growing, and the food (khachapuri, khinkali) alone makes the trip worthwhile. Internet is reliable. The main trade-off: limited direct flights compared to major European hubs.

Sofia, Bulgaria: $800 to $1,300 a month. The cheapest capital city in the EU, period. Bulgaria's digital nomad visa requires about EUR 950 a month in income, one of the lowest thresholds in Europe. The mountains are 30 minutes from the city center, the internet is fast, and your money goes roughly twice as far as it does in Lisbon. The downside: winter is cold and gray, and the city is not as polished as Western European alternatives.

Split, Croatia: $1,200 to $1,800 a month. Croatia's digital nomad visa gives you one year (not renewable, but you can reapply after six months outside the country). Split has the Adriatic coastline, Roman ruins, and a remote work community that centers on a few key coworking spaces. Summers are packed with tourists. Come in shoulder season (May or September) and you will have a much better experience.

San Jose, Costa Rica: $1,300 to $2,000 a month. Costa Rica's digital nomad visa requires $3,000 a month in income (or $4,000 for families), but it comes with a real perk: exemption from Costa Rican income tax. The visa is valid for one year, renewable for a second. The beaches are excellent, the internet in San Jose and the Central Valley is solid, and the country is safe and open to foreigners. The trade-off: import taxes make electronics and cars expensive.

Tokyo, Japan: $1,800 to $2,800 a month. Japan does not have a dedicated digital nomad visa yet, but the 90-day tourist visa is the starting point. The yen is at multi-decade lows against the dollar and euro, which makes Tokyo cheaper than it has been in years. Reliable infrastructure, the best public transit I have ever used, and a food scene that ranges from $5 ramen to Michelin stars. Nagasaki is also worth watching: the city launched a residency program in 2026 to attract remote workers to a lesser-known part of Japan.
