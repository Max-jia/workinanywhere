Most first-time nomads pack too much. I did. You imagine every scenario: the fancy dinner, the hiking trip, the client presentation, the rainy day. And you pack for all of them. Then you carry that overstuffed bag up five flights of stairs in a building without an elevator in 35 degrees, and you immediately understand why experienced nomads travel light.

This is the packing list I wish someone had handed me before my first year on the road. What to bring, what to skip, and the things you only learn after your third repack.

---

## The core principle: pack for 7-10 days

If you can do laundry once a week (you can), you need 7-10 days of clothing. Not 30. Not "just in case." Seven to ten. After three years of doing this, I can confirm you need a lot less than you think.

---

## Electronics: what you actually need

Your electronics are your livelihood. This category earns its weight.

### Essentials
- **Laptop**: The choice that has the biggest impact on your daily life. Battery life, portability, and a good keyboard matter more than raw power unless your work demands it (video editing, 3D rendering).
- **Noise-canceling headphones**: Not a luxury. Infrastructure. For focus in cafes and coworking spaces, for clear calls, for blocking out construction noise and hostel common rooms. Over-ear (Sony WH-1000XM6, $449) or in-ear (AirPods Pro 2, $249; Sony WF-1000XM6, ~$300) depending on what feels right to you.
- **Unlocked phone**: Dual SIM (physical + eSIM) is ideal. You will swap local SIM cards in every new country.
- **Universal power adapter**: One with multiple USB-C and USB-A ports. Skip the single-country adapters.
- **Portable battery / power bank**: 20,000 mAh minimum. Charges your phone 3-4 times. I have used mine on long bus rides, during airport delays, and in cafes with no accessible outlets. All three happen regularly.
- **External hard drive or SSD for backups**: If your laptop dies or gets stolen, you need to keep working. Cloud backup (Backblaze, iCloud, Google Drive) plus a physical backup drive.

### Nice to have, but consider the weight
- **Laptop stand**: The Roost V3 (~$89) folds flat. Raises your screen to eye level. Your neck and shoulders will notice the difference within a week.
- **Compact external keyboard and mouse**: Only if you work long hours. A folding Bluetooth keyboard weighs almost nothing.
- **Kindle or e-reader**: Lighter than books, battery lasts weeks. Better for reading than your phone or laptop, especially outdoors.
- **Small USB-C hub**: For connecting to external monitors, projectors, or wired internet when you need to.

### What to skip
- **Tablet (unless it is your primary device)**: Redundant if you have a laptop and phone. Extra weight, extra thing to charge, extra thing to worry about.
- **Second monitor**: Too heavy, too fragile. Use the external monitor at your coworking space or accommodation instead.
- **Gaming console**: Too heavy. Cloud gaming services exist if you really need to game.

## Clothing: the 7-10 day rule

### The formula
- 5-7 t-shirts / tops (merino wool if you can afford it: odor-resistant, dries fast, wears longer between washes)
- 2-3 button-downs or nicer tops (for video calls, client meetings, going out)
- 2-3 pairs of pants (one jeans, one lightweight, one versatile)
- 1-2 pairs of shorts
- 7-10 pairs of underwear and socks (merino wool socks are worth the money)
- 1 lightweight jacket or hoodie (airplanes, air-conditioned cafes, cool evenings)
- 1 rain shell (if you are going anywhere with actual rain)
- 1 pair of versatile sneakers (walking, light hiking, casual wear)
- 1 pair of sandals or flip-flops
- 1 pair of "nice enough" shoes (if your lifestyle requires them; otherwise, skip)

### What experienced nomads actually wear
After a few months on the road, most of us end up dressing the same way. Stripes, neutrals, solids. Everything matches everything else. If a shirt only goes with one specific pair of pants, leave it at home. You are optimizing for combinations, not outfits.

## Toiletries and health

- Prescription medications for your full trip duration (plus copies of prescriptions)
- Basic first-aid kit: painkillers, antihistamines, antidiarrheal, band-aids, antiseptic cream
- Sunscreen and insect repellent (surprisingly expensive or hard to find in some regions)
- Minimal toiletries: you can buy shampoo, soap, toothpaste, and deodorant anywhere

---

## Documents: digital and physical copies

- Passport (with at least 6 months validity and plenty of blank pages)
- Printed copies of passport (stored separately from your actual passport)
- Digital copies of everything on encrypted cloud storage: passport, visas, insurance policy, vaccination records, driver's license, birth certificate
- Extra passport photos (some visas and applications require physical photos)
- Travel insurance card / policy number saved offline on your phone
- List of emergency contacts (including your country's embassy or consulate in each destination)

## The items you will thank yourself for later

Small, cheap, and I use every one of these on every trip:

- **Earplugs** (hostel dorm, thin apartment walls, barking dogs, roosters, construction)
- **Sleep mask** (jet lag recovery, inconsistent curtains, early sunrises)
- **Packing cubes** (the single best quality-of-life improvement for frequent travelers: keeps your bag organized, makes repacking trivial, lets you find things without emptying everything)
- **Combination lock** (hostel lockers, gym lockers, securing your bag in transit)
- **Reusable water bottle** (with a filter if you are going anywhere with questionable tap water)
- **Compact towel** (microfiber, quick-dry: many budget accommodations do not provide towels)
- **Laundry line or dry bag** (for hand-washing clothes in sinks: saves money, solves the "no laundry nearby" problem)

## What I wish I had not brought

From my own mistakes and what I hear from other nomads, here are the most common packing regrets:

1. **Too many shoes.** Shoes are heavy, bulky, and you will wear the same one pair 90% of the time.
2. **"Just in case" formal wear.** Unless you have a specific event in your calendar (wedding, conference), you do not need a suit, dress shoes, or formal dress. If something comes up, you can buy or rent locally.
3. **Books.** Switch to an e-reader or phone app. Books are dead weight.
4. **Full-size toiletries.** Buy them when you arrive. Travel-size for the first few days only.
5. **Too much productivity gear.** You do not need a portable monitor, a mechanical keyboard, a vertical mouse, a laptop stand, and a separate webcam. Start with the essentials. Add only what you actually miss.
6. **Souvenirs and gifts from previous destinations.** Mail them home or do not buy them. Carrying gifts for people you might not see for months is unnecessary weight.

---

## One final rule

If you are not sure whether to bring something, do not bring it. You can buy almost anything you need locally. And if you cannot, you probably do not need it. Every item you do not pack is weight you do not carry up stairs, space you do not manage in a small rental, and one less thing to lose or break.

Pack for the life you actually live, not the life you imagine you might live.
