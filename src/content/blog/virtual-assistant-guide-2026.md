Most people who hear "virtual assistant" picture someone booking flights and ordering coffee for a CEO. I have hired and worked alongside VAs for years, and that version of the job is a small slice of what the work involves now.

The bigger picture: companies of every size now hire remote assistants to handle customer support, email management, social media scheduling, data entry, bookkeeping, and project coordination. The demand keeps growing because hiring a VA costs a fraction of a full-time onsite employee. The barrier to entry is about as low as it gets in remote work.

You do not need a degree. You do not need experience. You need to be organized, responsive, and willing to learn a few basic tools. I have seen people with zero resume land clients in their first week, and I have watched overqualified people fail because they could not reply to an email within three hours.

---

## What virtual assistants actually do

VA work splits into a few common categories. Most people start in one and branch out.

**Administrative support.** Email management, calendar scheduling, travel booking, data entry, file organization. This is where most VAs begin, and where the most consistent work lives. The tools you need: Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, and maybe Notion or Asana for task management.

**Customer service.** Answering emails, handling live chat, processing returns and refunds. Companies outsource this work aggressively, which means high demand but also high turnover. If you can write clear English and stay calm under pressure, you are qualified. I would not make this a whole career, but it is a reliable way to get your foot in the door.

**Social media management.** Scheduling posts, responding to comments, writing captions. Small business owners hate doing this themselves, which makes it one of the easiest services to sell. Basic Canva skills help here.

**E-commerce support.** Processing orders on Shopify, managing inventory, responding to customer questions about shipping. More small brands are going direct-to-consumer, and every one of them eventually hits a point where they cannot handle customer emails alone. That is your opening.

**Specialized services.** Bookkeeping, podcast editing, email marketing, funnel building. These pay more because most VAs do not offer them, so supply is low and clients do not comparison shop as aggressively. The tools take a weekend to learn.

## How much virtual assistants make

The range is wide. In mid-2026, the market breaks down like this:

Beginner VAs on Upwork typically charge $10 to $18 an hour while building reviews. After ten to fifteen completed jobs with strong feedback, the rate climbs to $20 to $30.

VAs who work through agencies like Belay and Time Etc make $18 to $25 an hour. The agency handles client acquisition, which is worth the pay cut when you are starting. Apply to two or three agencies at once. Their onboarding timelines vary wildly and you do not want to wait six weeks for a placement that never comes.

Independent VAs who find their own clients and specialize in something specific, like email marketing or e-commerce operations, charge $35 to $60 an hour. This is where the career tops out without moving into a different role.

A realistic first-year income for a full-time VA is $28,000 to $40,000. After two years, with repeat clients and a specialty, $50,000 to $70,000 is common. This is not get-rich-quick money. It is steady, location-independent income that you control.

## Where to find VA jobs

**Upwork** is the fastest path to your first paid work. The platform has a category dedicated to virtual assistance. Set your profile to highlight reliability and communication. Apply to five to ten jobs a day for the first week. Take anything that gets you a review, even if the pay is low. After three positive reviews, raise your rate. I have watched people triple their hourly rate on Upwork within six months by stacking good reviews and being easy to work with.

**Belay** hires VAs for administrative, bookkeeping, and web support roles. They match you with clients and handle billing. The application process includes skills testing and an interview. Once placed, the work is steady. Hourly rates start around $18. The pay is fair, not great. The steady stream of work makes it worth it while you build an independent client base on the side.

**Time Etc** works similarly to Belay. They specialize in administrative and marketing support. Founded in 2007, the company has been around longer than most and has a large client base. Application requirements include relevant experience, which for a brand new VA can mean volunteer work, managing a household, or helping a friend's business. Do not overthink this. If you have organized anything for anyone, you have relevant experience.

**Fancy Hands** offers one-off tasks rather than ongoing clients: phone calls, research, data entry. The pay is low, around $5 to $12 per task. The barrier to entry is nonexistent. Complete a few dozen tasks and you have something real to put on your profile.

**Direct outreach.** Pick a niche, like e-commerce store support or real estate admin. Find small business owners who clearly need help. Send a short email offering a free trial morning. Of the dozen-plus VAs I have hired, the best one came from a cold email: three sentences, a clear offer, and a link to their portfolio. Most business owners will not reply. You only need one yes.

## What you need to get started

A reliable laptop and internet connection. That gets you 90 percent of the way.

A quiet workspace. You will spend hours on calls, and a background that broadcasts your entire life to clients is a fast way to lose their confidence. I have passed on hiring someone because their video call sounded like a daycare center.

A professional profile photo. Not a selfie. Not an AI-generated headshot. A clear photo of you looking like someone a business owner would trust with their inbox.

Basic proficiency with Google Workspace, which you can learn from free tutorials in a weekend.

A simple contract template for freelance work. Grab one from Bonsai or HelloSign. Do not start working without one. Every freelancer I know has a story about a client who ghosted on a large invoice. A contract is cheap protection.

---

## The biggest thing nobody tells you

This is the thing I care about most when hiring. Client communication is the job. More than any technical skill, more than any tool. Clients hire VAs because they are overwhelmed and need someone who makes things simpler, not someone who needs handholding.

Answer messages within a few hours. Confirm you received a task before starting it. If something will take longer than expected, say so immediately. If you do not know how to do something, say "I will figure it out" instead of "I do not know how." Then figure it out.

The VAs I have kept for years communicate like this. The ones I replaced after two weeks are the ones who went silent when something went wrong.
