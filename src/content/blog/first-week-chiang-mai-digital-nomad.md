Every guide calls Chiang Mai the perfect nomad city. Cheap rent, great food, friendly community. What they skip is the part between landing at CNX and actually feeling settled. I found that first week disorienting. It is also when you make the decisions that determine whether you stick around or leave after a month.

I have watched too many people arrive, book a year-long lease from a Facebook listing they never visited, and end up in a neighborhood they hate. Do not be that person.

---

## Day 1: Land. Do not make decisions.

You just crossed eight time zones. You are tired. Everything smells different, sounds different, looks different. You will want to be productive and start apartment hunting immediately. I tried this once in another city. Bad move. You make worse decisions when you are exhausted than you realize.

Get a SIM card at the airport. AIS or True, 200 to 600 baht for a month of data. Bring your passport. The kiosk staff handles the setup. It took me five minutes.

Withdraw cash. 5,000 to 10,000 baht, small bills. Street food stalls, songthaews, and markets are cash only. The ATM will offer to charge you in your home currency. Say no. Always pick local currency. The bank's dynamic conversion tacks on 3 to 5 percent for absolutely no reason.

Install Grab. It is Thailand's Uber. You will use it until you learn the songthaew system, probably for weeks after that.

Go to your short-term accommodation. You booked four to six nights, not a three-month lease. Good. Eat something. Sleep. The city will still be here tomorrow.

---

## Days 2-3: Walk the sois

Rentals here work differently from what you are used to. The best apartments never show up online. Landlords tape yellow or red signs with phone numbers on their gates and wait for someone to knock. The stuff on Airbnb and Facebook groups? Mostly leftovers that locals passed on.

Walk the sois in Nimmanhaemin. Walk the sois in Santitham. When you see a sign, take a photo of the phone number and call or message. Most landlords speak enough English to set up a viewing. If they do not, just send a message in English. They use Google Translate. It works fine.

View at least five apartments before you commit. Test the water pressure. Run a WiFi speed test inside the unit, not in the lobby. Visit the street at different times of day. A quiet soi at 11am can be a karaoke bar at 10pm.

Do not rent a scooter yet. You do not know the traffic flow or which intersections are dangerous. I have seen too many tourists in bandages. Wait until week two.

---

## Days 4-5: Negotiate like a local

Revisit your top two or three apartments. See them again. Then negotiate.

Landlords here expect you to haggle. If you are standing in front of them with cash and a passport, you have leverage. I used the line "I can sign today. What is your real price?" and got 20 percent off. It usually gets you somewhere between 15 and 25 percent off.

DTV visa holders get better deals. Landlords know you are staying six to twelve months. Mention it. Long-term tenants are rare and landlords value that.

The contract will be in Thai. Use Google Translate. The key terms are rent amount, deposit (usually one month), notice period, and whether utilities are included. Get these confirmed in writing, even if it is just a WhatsApp message in English.

---

## Day 6: Set up your infrastructure

Open a Bangkok Bank or Kasikorn account. You need your passport and a residence certificate from Immigration. Your landlord files a TM30 form within 24 hours of your arrival. Ask them to do this on day one. Some landlords drag their feet on this. Be persistent.

Tour the coworking spaces. Punspace, MANA, Hub53, Yellow. I bought a monthly pass at the first one I visited and regretted it. Try day passes at two or three first. The vibe matters more than the amenities, and each space attracts a different crowd.

Find your local. The coffee shop you return to every morning, the street food stall where the owner starts remembering your order, the market where you buy fruit. When everything else is new, these small routines keep you grounded.

---

## Day 7: Eat khao soi

Not pad thai. Khao soi. It is the dish of northern Thailand, a coconut curry noodle soup with crispy noodles on top. Go to Khao Soi Khun Yai near the Old City. They sell out by 1pm, sometimes earlier. The first bowl I ate there is still the best meal I have had in Chiang Mai. It is not just food. It is the moment you stop being a visitor and start being someone who lives here.

## FAQ

**Do I need a visa for Chiang Mai?**
Most Western passport holders get 60 days visa-free on arrival, extendable by 30 days at the immigration office for 1,900 THB ($54). The Destination Thailand Visa (DTV) allows stays up to 180 days for remote workers and is the best long-term option. For the full picture, see our [guide to 50+ nomad visas](/blog/50-countries-nomad-visas-2026).

**What is the best neighborhood for digital nomads?**
Nimmanhaemin is the default. Coworking spaces, cafes, and the largest expat concentration. Santitham is cheaper, quieter, and more local. The Old City is charming but more touristy. Try Nimman first. If you want more local flavor, walk Santitham on day two.

**Do I need to speak Thai?**
No. You can live comfortably in Chiang Mai with English. Learn hello (sawadee khrap/kha), thank you (khop khun khrap/kha), and numbers one through ten. That gets you 90 percent of the way. Anything beyond that earns you real goodwill from locals.
